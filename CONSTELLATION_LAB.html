01

What a wave is

We have to start here, because everything else is built on it. If you already know this, it takes thirty seconds to skim.

A wave is something that wiggles up and down, over and over, in a regular pattern. A guitar string. Ripples on water. And, importantly for you, light.

If you draw a wave on paper — height on the vertical axis, time going across — you get that familiar smooth up-and-down shape. Mathematically it is a sine wave, and you can describe any one of them completely with just three numbers:

NumberWhat it meansEveryday example
AmplitudeHow tall the wiggle isHow loud a sound is
FrequencyHow fast it wiggles — wiggles per secondHow high-pitched a sound is
PhaseWhere in its cycle it is right nowTwo singers slightly out of time with each other

Amplitude and frequency are usually easy to grasp. Phase is the one that trips people up, so here is the clearest way to think about it.

Phase, explained properly

Imagine a point going round and round a circle at a steady speed, like the tip of a clock's second hand.

Now watch only its height above the centre, and plot that height against time. What you get is exactly a sine wave — it goes up, comes back down, goes below, comes back. One full lap of the circle equals one full wiggle.

Phase is simply where the hand is pointing right now. Straight up, to the right, at 45 degrees. It is an angle, measured in degrees or radians.

So a wave is a spinning arrow. The length of the arrow is the amplitude. The angle of the arrow is the phase. How fast it spins is the frequency.

02

Putting data on a wave

A computer stores everything as bits — ones and zeros. To send them somewhere you need to physically change something the far end can measure. Changing a property of a wave to carry information is called modulation.

Attempt 1: switch it on and off

Light on = 1. Light off = 0. Simple, and it is how the earliest fibre systems worked. But each flash carries only one bit, so to go faster you must flash faster, and there is a limit to how fast the hardware can flash.

Attempt 2: use several brightness levels

Instead of just on and off, use four brightness levels: off, dim, medium, bright. Now each flash carries two bits (00, 01, 10, 11) — twice the data at the same flash rate.

But brightness levels are fragile. A little noise and "dim" looks like "medium".

Attempt 3: use the phase as well

Here is the clever part. The receiver can measure not only how bright the light is, but also where in its cycle it arrived — its phase.

So now you have two independent dials to set: brightness and phase. Instead of a handful of brightness levels, you can have a whole grid of brightness-and-phase combinations.

The core idea of your whole field

Each distinct combination of amplitude and phase is called a symbol. The complete set of symbols a system uses is its modulation format.

Sixteen different symbols means each one carries four bits, because 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16.

Think of it as an alphabet. An alphabet with 4 letters, or 16 letters, or 64. More letters means each one carries more meaning — but they also get harder to tell apart.

03

Two numbers: I and Q

Describing a symbol as "amplitude 0.7, phase 135 degrees" is correct but awkward to work with. Engineers use a different pair of numbers that carry exactly the same information and are far easier to handle.

Go back to the spinning arrow. Instead of describing it by its length and angle, describe it by how far right it reaches and how far up it reaches. Its shadow on each axis.

  • I — the horizontal part. Short for in-phase.
  • Q — the vertical part. Short for quadrature, an old word meaning "at 90 degrees".

Those two names are pure historical baggage. Just read them as "horizontal" and "vertical".

Why bother switching?

Because angles are painful in maths. Adding two waves means adding their angles in a way that is not straightforward.

In I–Q form you just add the horizontals and add the verticals. Noise, which comes in from all directions equally, becomes simple to describe. And a receiver's hardware naturally produces I and Q directly — that is genuinely what comes out of the detector.

You already saw I and Q in Lab 1 — the two numbers at the bottom of the readout. Go back and drag the phase slider while watching them. When the arrow points right, I is large and Q is zero. Point it straight up and the opposite happens.

If you remember one thing from this section

Every symbol is just a point on a flat 2-D plane. Its horizontal position is I, its vertical position is Q. That is all a symbol is.

04

The constellation diagram

If one symbol is a point on a plane, then the whole alphabet is a pattern of points.

Draw all the allowed points on one graph — I across, Q up — and you have a constellation diagram. It is called that because a scatter of dots on a dark background genuinely looks like a star chart.

This picture is the single most important object in your project, for one reason:

Why your paper exists at all

Different modulation formats produce visibly different patterns. QPSK makes four dots. 16-QAM makes a 4×4 grid. 64-QAM makes an 8×8 grid.

So "which format is this?" becomes "which pattern is this?" — and that is a picture-recognition problem, which computers are extremely good at.

In a real receiver you do not get one clean point per symbol. You get thousands of arriving symbols, each landing slightly off its intended spot because of noise. So a real constellation diagram is a cloud of thousands of dots, clustered around where the ideal points should be.

The five alphabets

Your project uses five formats. Click through them below. These are the ideal points, with no noise at all.

Lab 2 — the five alphabetsclick a format

Watch the spacing as you go up. QPSK's four points are far apart and easy to distinguish. 64-QAM crams 64 points into the same area, so they sit close together and a small nudge from noise can push one into its neighbour's territory.

Reading the names

QAM stands for Quadrature Amplitude Modulation — it just means "we vary both amplitude and phase". The number in front is how many points there are.

QPSK stands for Quadrature Phase Shift Keying. It has four points all at the same distance from the centre, so only the phase changes, never the brightness. It is really 4-QAM by another name.

The trade-off, in one table

FormatPointsBits eachCharacter
QPSK42Slowest, but survives a very noisy line
8-QAM83A star, not a grid
16-QAM164The common workhorse
32-QAM325A cross — see below
64-QAM646Fastest, but needs a clean line

Why two of them are odd shapes

16 and 64 are perfect squares (4×4 and 8×8), so those formats form neat square grids. 8 and 32 are not perfect squares, so you cannot make a square grid out of them.

  • 8-QAM uses a star: four points on an inner ring and four on an outer ring, rotated so they interleave.
  • 32-QAM uses a cross: take a 6×6 grid (36 points) and delete the four corners, leaving exactly 32.
Why this matters for your paper

Including two non-square shapes was a deliberate choice. A classifier that only ever sees square grids of different densities has an artificially easy job — it could almost get by counting.

Adding a star and a cross forces it to recognise genuinely different shapes, which makes your result harder to dismiss. If a reviewer asks why you did not just use three square formats, that is your answer.

06

Decibels, briefly

Every measurement in your paper is in decibels (dB), so it is worth thirty seconds on what they are.

A decibel is not a fixed amount of anything. It is a way of writing a ratio — how many times bigger one thing is than another — on a squashed scale.

Signal power in communications ranges over enormous factors: a million times, a billion times. Writing those numbers out is unwieldy. Decibels compress them:

DecibelsMeans the ratio is
0 dB1× — equal
3 dBabout 2×
10 dB10×
20 dB100×
30 dB1000×

Two consequences worth internalising:

  • Every 3 dB doubles the ratio. So 25 dB is not "a bit more" than 5 dB — it is a hundred times more.
  • Adding decibels means multiplying the underlying ratio. 10 dB + 10 dB = 20 dB, which is 10× × 10× = 100×.

When your paper says the CNN gains a "7 dB margin", that means it copes with a signal five times weaker than the classical method needs. That is why engineers quote margins in dB rather than percentages — it maps directly onto how much further the light can travel.

07

Noise, and where it comes from

If the picture always looked as clean as Lab 2, this would be a solved problem and there would be no paper.

Why light needs help along the way

Glass is very transparent but not perfectly so. Over a long distance the light fades. To cross an ocean you have to boost it back up roughly every 80 kilometres, using a device called an optical amplifier (an EDFA, if you want the acronym — erbium-doped fibre amplifier).

Here is the problem. An amplifier cannot boost only your signal. The physics of amplification always adds a small amount of random extra light of its own, called ASE — amplified spontaneous emission.

Cross a continent and your signal has been through dozens of amplifiers, each adding a little more randomness. It accumulates and never goes away.

What noise does to the picture

Each arriving symbol gets nudged a small random distance from where it should be. With thousands of symbols, each ideal point becomes a fuzzy cloud. More noise means bigger clouds. Eventually neighbouring clouds overlap, and you can no longer tell which point a symbol came from.

OSNR — the measure of how bad it has got

OSNR means Optical Signal-to-Noise Ratio. It is simply how strong your signal is compared with the accumulated noise, quoted in decibels.

  • High OSNR (25 dB) — clean line, tight clusters
  • Low OSNR (5 dB) — noisy line, overlapping mush

Every result in your paper is plotted against OSNR. Drag the slider and watch it happen.

Try this before moving on

Set the OSNR to 5 dB. Now click between 16-QAM and 64-QAM.

You cannot tell them apart. Neither can any classifier, human or machine — the difference has been physically erased by the channel.

This is why accuracy falls at low OSNR in your results, and it is a law of physics, not a flaw in your model. Being able to say that confidently is worth marks.

One subtlety worth knowing

OSNR is measured by an optical instrument over a fixed standard slice of the spectrum. The electrical signal-to-noise ratio the receiver's circuitry actually experiences is slightly different, because the receiver filters the signal down to its own bandwidth.

For your system the relationship is SNR = OSNR − 1.07 dB. Your paper does this conversion properly, which many student projects skip. It is a good detail to have ready.

08

Imperfect hardware

Noise is not the only enemy. Real equipment is imperfect, and it distorts the picture in ways that are different in kind from noise. That distinction turns out to be the heart of your paper.

The four imperfections your simulator models

ImperfectionPhysical causeEffect on the picture
Laser phase noiseA laser's phase drifts randomly; it is never perfectly steadySmearing around the circle
Frequency offsetThe sender's laser and the receiver's laser are separate devices and never match exactlyThe whole picture slowly rotates

I/Q phase skewThe optical component that splits I from Q should give exactly 90°, but does notThe picture is sheared

Concentrate on the two highlighted rows. They are the ones that matter for your result, and they share a property:

The distinction your paper rests on

Noise blurs. It spreads each point into a fuzzy cloud but leaves the overall shape where it was.

I/Q imbalance distorts the geometry. It moves the points to the wrong places — stretching and shearing the whole pattern.

These are not the same thing, and as you are about to see, the two approaches in your paper cope with them very differently.

09

The problem your paper solves

Now that you know what the pictures are, here is the actual engineering problem.

The receiver must know which alphabet is arriving before it can read anything. The same received point means different things in different formats — in QPSK there are only four possibilities, in 64-QAM there are sixty-four. Decode with the wrong assumption and you get nonsense.

Think of it this way

Someone starts speaking to you. Before you can understand a single word you need to know whether they are speaking Hindi, English or Tamil.

Traditionally the sender phones ahead: "I am about to speak Hindi." That works, but it needs a second phone line kept open just for announcements.

It would be far better if you could simply listen for a moment and work it out yourself.

That is Modulation Format Identification — MFI. Your project's entire subject.

Why anyone cares

Modern networks are elastic: they change format on the fly to squeeze maximum capacity from each route. A crowded fast alphabet on a short clean city link; a simple robust one on a long undersea cable. If a link degrades, the network downshifts automatically.

All that switching should happen without human involvement, and automatic switching needs automatic recognition. MFI is one piece of a broader job called Optical Performance Monitoring — judging a link's health purely from the signal arriving at the far end.

10

How a machine learns

Switching subjects entirely for a moment. If you already know what a neural network is, skip to section 11.

Ordinary programming versus learning

Normally you program a computer by writing the rules yourself: if this, then that. That works when you can state the rules.

But for something like "is this a 16-QAM or a 64-QAM picture?", writing the rules by hand is painfully hard. So instead you use machine learning: show the computer thousands of examples with the right answers attached, and let it work out the rules itself.

What "training" actually means

A model starts with random internal settings and is therefore useless. Then, over and over:

  1. Show it one example
  2. Let it guess
  3. Compare its guess with the right answer
  4. Nudge every internal setting a tiny amount in whichever direction would have made the guess better

Repeat a few hundred thousand times and the settings drift into a configuration that gets most examples right. That is all training is. There is no understanding involved — just a very large number of tiny corrections.

The three sets of data, and why

Training set — what the model learns from. 70% of your data.

Validation set — checked during training to decide when to stop. Because you make decisions using it, it stops being a neutral judge. 15%.

Test set — touched exactly once, right at the end. This is the only honest number, and it is what goes in the paper. 15%.

If someone asks why you need three rather than two, that middle paragraph is the answer.

What a neural network is

Imagine a large number of very simple units, each of which does something trivial: take several numbers in, multiply each by a weight, add them up, and pass the result on.

Stack thousands of those in layers, connect them, and let training adjust all the weights. That is a neural network. The "deep" in deep learning just means "with many layers".

What a CNN is, and why it suits pictures

A Convolutional Neural Network is a neural network specialised for images. The key trick is this.

Instead of looking at the whole image at once, it slides a small window — typically 3×3 pixels — across the picture, applying the same small set of weights at every position.

Why sliding the same window everywhere is clever

If the network learns what a bright blob looks like, that knowledge works everywhere in the image automatically, because the same weights are reused at every position.

A network without this trick would have to learn what a blob looks like separately at all 4,096 pixel positions. This is why CNNs need far fewer settings than you would expect — yours has only about 286,000, which is tiny by modern standards.

Stack several of these layers and something useful happens: early layers detect simple things (edges, blobs), later layers detect arrangements of those things (a grid, a cross). Which is exactly what distinguishing constellation formats requires.

11

The old way: 15 numbers

Before deep learning, engineers solved MFI differently. Rather than look at the picture, they measured a handful of statistics from the signal and fed those to a simple classifier.

Your paper uses 15 such numbers, because that is what the classical literature uses. The important ones are called higher-order cumulants.

Cumulants, without the maths

Start with something familiar. Given a pile of numbers you can compute the average. You can also compute the average of the squares, the average of the cubes, and so on. These are called moments, and together they describe the shape of the distribution.

Cumulants are specific combinations of moments, put together so that something remarkable happens: the contribution of random Gaussian noise cancels out at fourth order and above.

That makes them a noise-resistant fingerprint. Each format has its own theoretical cumulant value, so in principle a few numbers identify the format even in a noisy signal. It is genuinely elegant, and it is why the technique dominated for two decades.

But notice the assumption hiding inside

Cumulants cancel Gaussian noise. They are computed assuming the I and Q measurements are balanced and well-behaved.

I/Q imbalance breaks that assumption. It is not noise — it moves the points to the wrong places. The cumulants shift, and the classifier has no idea anything is wrong.

You saw this happen in Lab 4. That is the whole story.

The two classical classifiers

SVM (Support Vector Machine). Draws a boundary between the classes, positioned so the empty gap on either side is as wide as possible. A wide gap tends to generalise well. Only the few examples nearest the boundary actually determine where it sits.

k-NN (k-Nearest Neighbours). Does no training at all. To classify something new it finds the 5 most similar past examples and takes a majority vote. It is the simplest sensible classifier that exists, which is exactly why it makes a good floor for comparison.

12

The new way: look at the picture

Since around 2017, the alternative has been to skip the statistics entirely: take the constellation image and hand it to a CNN.

It works well, and the literature agrees it beats the classical approach. That much is not in dispute, and your paper does not dispute it.

How your images are made

You do not save a scatter plot. You lay a 64×64 grid over the I–Q plane and count how many symbols land in each cell. That count becomes the pixel brightness. It is a 2-D histogram.

Two reasons this is better than a scatter plot:

  • Speed. Saving 63,000 plot images would take about 50 minutes. Counting into a grid takes seconds — which is what made it practical to iterate on your experiments at all.
  • More information. A scatter plot saturates to solid colour where dots pile up, throwing away density. The histogram keeps it — and density is exactly what separates 16-QAM from 64-QAM when they are noisy.

There is also a small but important detail: the brightness is stored on a logarithmic scale. Cluster centres get vastly more hits than the sparse edges, so on a linear scale the edges would round to zero and vanish. The log scale lifts the faint detail. It is the same reason astronomy images use one.

Your research question

Everything so far has been background. This section is your actual contribution, and it is worth reading twice.

When researchers compared the classical method against the deep learning method, they changed two things at the same time:

Classical approachDeep learning approach
The methodsimple classifierneural network

The deep learning approach wins. But which of the two changes caused it? Nobody separated them.

Think of it this way

You want to know whether a new pair of spectacles helps you read. So you put them on — but at the same moment you also switch on a much brighter lamp.

Your reading improves. Was it the spectacles, or the lamp?

You cannot possibly tell. To find out, you would have to try the spectacles under the old dim lamp.

The missing experiment, which you built

You added a classifier nobody usually includes: a neural network that sees only the 15 numbers. New spectacles, old dim lamp.

Once you have it, the logic is forced:

  • If it scores like the old simple method → the lamp mattered. What you show the model is what counts.
  • If it scores like the picture-based network → the spectacles mattered. The method is what counts.

Either result is a real finding, which is what makes this a research question rather than a demonstration.

14

What you found

It was the lamp.

Your paper's claim, in one sentence

What you show the model matters roughly three times more than how clever the model is.

Why the raw-signal model did worst

Look at the leftmost bar. The IQ-CNN sees the raw samples — nothing was thrown away at all — and it performs worst. That surprises people.

The explanation is the most satisfying part of your project. The answer depends on the distribution of the arriving points: where they cluster, how densely, in what arrangement. But raw samples arrive as a list in time order, and the symbols are independent, so their order carries almost no information about the format.

The network has to work out a distribution from a list. The constellation image hands that distribution over directly — a picture built by counting how many points land in each square is a map of the distribution. The binning step does real work the network would otherwise have to learn for itself.

The exception, and why it makes your paper better

Under heavy chromatic dispersion the classical method retains 80.1% against the CNN's 54.9%. Your method does not win everywhere.

And it fits the same mechanism: dispersion broadens the cloud rather like added noise — and cumulants are specifically built so Gaussian noise cancels out. So the exception is predicted by the explanation, not a contradiction of it.

Your paper reports this openly. A result that qualifies its own claim is far more believable than one that wins every single panel. If an examiner asks whether your method is always better, this is your answer — and giving it will do you more good than claiming a clean sweep.

Result 4 — and it cannot tell you it is failing

An accuracy number is something only you, the experimenter, can see. A monitor running in a real network knows only what it predicted and how confident it felt.

So your final experiment asked: when the model is wrong, does it know?

The classical method claims to be almost twice as accurate as it actually is.

The classical monitor can be made safe — but only by silencing it. At that setting it answers two inputs in every thousand. That is not a monitor; it is an off switch.

The image-based one keeps working: 87% coverage at 97% accuracy, on a channel it was never trained for.

The whole argument, end to end

Read as one sentence, your paper says:

What you show the model matters more than what the model is → which is why the classical method collapses when the channel differs from training → caused specifically by I/Q imbalance corrupting the statistics → and it cannot even tell you that it is happening.

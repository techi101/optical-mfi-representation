01

Why light needs an alphabet

The internet does not travel through the air. It travels as pulses of laser light inside strands of glass thinner than a human hair, buried under streets and laid across ocean floors.

To carry data on that light, we make it wiggle in specific, agreed-upon patterns. Each distinct pattern stands for a chunk of bits. A complete set of patterns is called a modulation format — but it is easier to think of it as an alphabet.

Several alphabets are in everyday use, and they trade off against each other:

AlphabetPatternsBits eachCharacter
QPSK42Slow, but survives a very noisy line
8-QAM83 
16-QAM164 
32-QAM325 
64-QAM646Fast, but needs a clean line

More patterns means more data per pulse. But the patterns then sit closer together, so it takes less interference to confuse one with another. That single trade-off drives this entire field.

The catch

The receiver must know which alphabet was used before it can decode anything at all. The same received pattern means different things in different alphabets.

Think of it this way

Someone begins speaking to you. Before you can understand a single word, you need to know whether they are speaking Hindi, English or Tamil.

Traditionally, the sender phones ahead: “I am about to speak Hindi.” That works — but it needs a second phone line kept open purely for announcements.

It would be far better if you could simply listen for a moment and work it out yourself.

That is the problem, and it has a name: Modulation Format Identification, or MFI. It sits inside the broader job of Optical Performance Monitoring — judging the health of a link purely from the signal arriving at the far end.

Why it matters commercially

Modern networks are elastic: they switch alphabets on the fly to squeeze the most capacity out of every route. A crowded fast alphabet on a short clean city link; a simple robust one on a long undersea route. If a link degrades, the network downshifts. All of that switching should be automatic, and automatic switching needs automatic recognition.

02

The constellation diagram

There is a standard way to draw what is arriving. It turns an invisible physics problem into a picture — which is precisely why machine learning can be pointed at it.

Light has two properties we can control independently: how bright it is and where it is in its wave cycle (its phase). Plot brightness-and-phase as a point on a graph, do that for every symbol that arrives, and you get a constellation diagram.

Think of it as a fingerprint of the alphabet:

The three pictures above are drawn to the same scale. Notice how much closer together the 64-QAM points sit — that crowding is exactly why it is fragile.

Two of the five alphabets in our work are deliberately not square grids: 8-QAM is a star (four inner points, four outer, rotated), and 32-QAM is a cross (a 6×6 grid with the four corners removed, because 32 is not a perfect square). We included them on purpose. A classifier that only ever sees square grids of varying density has an artificially easy job, and a reviewer would notice.

The idea in one line

If the alphabet is a picture, then identifying the alphabet is an image-recognition problem — and we know how to do those.

03

What noise does to it

If the picture always looked as clean as the diagrams above, this would be a solved problem and there would be no paper. It does not.

Light loses power as it travels through glass, so every 80 km or so an amplifier boosts it back up. But an amplifier cannot boost only the signal — it also adds a small amount of its own random light. Cross a continent and you have passed through dozens of amplifiers, each adding a little more.

The measure of how bad this has become is OSNR, the Optical Signal-to-Noise Ratio, quoted in decibels. High OSNR means a clean signal; low OSNR means a noisy one. Every result in our project is plotted against it.

Here is what it actually does — these are real images from our simulator:

Look at the bottom-left corner. At 5 dB, 32-QAM and 64-QAM are both featureless clouds. They are not hard to tell apart — they are genuinely impossible. The information has been physically destroyed by the channel.

Why this matters for your viva

When accuracy falls at low OSNR, that is not a weakness of our model. No classifier — and no human expert — can recover information the channel has erased. It is a fundamental limit, and being able to say so confidently is the difference between defending your work and apologising for it.

04

The two ways people solve this

Two approaches exist, from two different eras.

The classical way (roughly 2000–2015)

Do not look at the picture. Instead, compute about 15 statistical numbers that summarise the signal — averages, spreads, and some more exotic quantities called higher-order cumulants. Feed those numbers to a straightforward classifier such as a Support Vector Machine.

Cumulants have a genuinely elegant property: they are built so that the contribution of random Gaussian noise mathematically cancels out. That makes them a noise-resistant fingerprint, and each alphabet has its own distinct theoretical value.

The deep-learning way (roughly 2017 onwards)

Take the actual constellation picture and feed it to a Convolutional Neural Network — the same family of model used for face recognition and medical imaging.

A CNN slides a small filter across the whole image, reusing the same filter everywhere. So if it learns what a bright blob looks like once, it recognises blobs at every position automatically. That reuse is why a CNN needs far fewer settings than a network that must learn each pixel position separately.

The published finding is consistent: the deep-learning way wins. That is not in dispute, and our work does not dispute it.

05

The gap nobody checked

This is our contribution. It is worth reading twice, because everything else in the project exists to support it.

When researchers compared the classical way against the deep-learning way, they changed two things at the same time:

Think of it this way

You want to know whether a new pair of spectacles helps you read. So you put them on — but at the same moment you also switch on a much brighter lamp.

Your reading improves. Was it the spectacles, or the lamp?

You cannot tell. To find out, you would have to try the spectacles under the old dim lamp.

That missing test is what we built.

We added a classifier that nobody usually includes: a neural network that only sees the 15 numbers. New spectacles, old dim lamp. Once you have it, the logic is forced:

  • If it scores like the old simple method → the lamp mattered. The input is what counts.
  • If it scores like the picture-based network → the spectacles mattered. The method is what counts.

Either answer is a real finding. That is what makes it a research question rather than a demonstration.

Our experiment

Five classifiers, arranged so that the method and the input can be varied independently. Every one of them is trained and tested on identical signals, so any difference is attributable to the design and not to luckier data.

ClassifierWhat it seesHuman pre-processingRole in the argument
CNN64×64 pictureBinning onlyDeep model, rich input
IQ-CNNRaw signal samplesNone at allDeep model, raw input
MLP15 numbersHeavyThe missing control
SVM15 numbersHeavyThe classical baseline
k-NN15 numbersHeavyThe simplest possible method

Read the table in two directions and it does two different jobs:

  • MLP vs SVM vs k-NN — same input, three fundamentally different algorithms (a trained network, a mathematical boundary-finder, and a simple “find the most similar past example” lookup). This isolates the effect of the method.
  • CNN vs MLP — both trained neural networks, different input. This isolates the effect of the representation.

The third row is the one that turns a project into a paper. Without it, the comparison stays confounded.

07

Building a fibre link in software

There is no dataset of optical constellation diagrams to download — free or paid. Producing one needs a laboratory worth crores of rupees, not a subscription.

So we wrote a program that behaves like a real fibre link. This is standard practice in the field, not a shortcut: even with unlimited money we would still simulate, because only simulation gives you precisely controlled noise levels and guaranteed correct labels.

What the simulator models

Most student projects add only random noise and stop. Ours also models the four imperfections that a real receiver genuinely has to live with:

ImperfectionPhysical causeEffect on the picture
Amplifier noiseAmplifiers add random light of their ownEvery point spreads into a cloud
Laser phase noiseA laser’s phase drifts as a random walkSmearing around the circle
Frequency offsetSender and receiver lasers never match exactlyThe whole picture slowly rotates
I/Q imbalanceThe two receiver arms have unequal gainThe grid is skewed, not blurred
Residual dispersionColours travel at slightly different speedsNeighbouring points bleed together

We can switch each one on and off independently, which is what makes the robustness experiment possible. Here is each imperfection in isolation, at high signal quality so you see the imperfection rather than the noise:

Proving the simulator is right

This is the part to be genuinely proud of, and the first thing to show a professor.

For a clean constellation, one of those cumulant values can be calculated exactly on paper — it is a finite sum over the constellation points, with no simulation involved. If our simulator is correct, it must reproduce those numbers. It does:

AlphabetExact theoryOur simulatorError
QPSK1.00000.99970.0003
8-QAM1.52741.52510.0023
16-QAM0.68000.68300.0030
32-QAM0.19000.18680.0032
64-QAM0.61900.61980.0007

Agreement to three or four decimal places, before any machine learning is involved. The three square alphabets additionally match the constants published in textbooks.

Say this in your viva

“The data is simulated, not invented. I validated the simulator against exact theory before training anything — the cumulants match to within 0.004, the noise power measures within 0.02 dB of target, and the noise is verifiably Gaussian and zero-mean.”

Most students who download a dataset cannot make any equivalent claim about theirs.

A bug worth telling them about

The first version applied the frequency offset after the receiver’s phase-tracking stage. That is wrong physics — cancelling that drift is precisely one of the things phase-tracking does. The result was that a tiny 1 MHz offset smeared every point into a 46-degree arc, and constellations looked destroyed even at excellent signal quality.

The fix was to apply it before phase-tracking, so the receiver removes the slow part and only the within-block wobble survives. Mentioning this shows you understand what the receiver’s signal processing actually does, rather than only how to call a function.

08

What we found

Final numbers, averaged over three independent runs. Everything below is measured, not estimated.

98.73CNNthe picture
93.41SVM15 numbers
93.08MLP15 numbers
91.58k-NN15 numbers
86.56IQ-CNNraw samples

Look at the middle three. A trained neural network, a mathematical boundary-finder, and a simple similarity lookup — three approaches with almost nothing in common — all land within 1.83 percentage points of each other when shown the same 15 numbers.

Now show a comparable network the full picture instead: 98.73%, a gain of 5.32 points over the best of them.

And there is a detail that sharpens it further. The MLP is a deep neural network and the SVM is not — yet the MLP came out slightly lower (93.08 against 93.41). Making the model deep, while feeding it the same 15 numbers, bought nothing at all.

The finding

Changing the algorithm moved accuracy by 1.83 points. Changing what the algorithm was shown moved it by 5.32 points — roughly three times as much.

What you show the model matters far more than how clever the model is.

The run-to-run spread was ±0.12, ±0.10, ±0.10 and ±0.08 for those four models. The 5.32-point gap is around forty times the noise, so it is not luck.

Stated as an OSNR margin

Percentages mean little to an optical engineer. Signal quality does. The OSNR needed to reach a target accuracy:

Modelfor 95% accuracyfor 99% accuracy
CNN (picture)7 dB10 dB
SVM (15 numbers)14 dB18 dB
k-NN (15 numbers)15 dB18 dB
The number to quote

The CNN reaches 95% accuracy at 7 dB where the classical method needs 14 dB — a 7 dB OSNR margin.

In optical terms that is enormous. It converts directly into longer reach, or fewer amplifiers on the route.

Why the raw-signal network did worst

This surprises people, because that model has access to the most information — nothing was thrown away at all. The explanation is the most satisfying part of the project.

The answer depends on the distribution of the arriving points: where they cluster, how densely, in what arrangement. But the raw samples arrive as a sequence in time, and the symbols are independent — so their order carries almost no information about the alphabet. The network must work out a distribution from a list.

The constellation picture hands that distribution over directly. A picture built by counting how many points land in each square is a map of the distribution. The binning step does real work that the network would otherwise have to learn for itself.

So the gain is not mysterious, and it is not about the CNN being clever. It is about giving the model something much closer to the quantity the answer actually depends on.

What else we tested

Four further experiments, each answering a question a reviewer will certainly ask:

ExperimentThe question it answers
Unseen noise levelsDoes it still work at signal qualities it never saw in training? Proves it learned the physics rather than memorising each level.
Architecture sweepDo our design choices actually matter, or did we just guess? Turns “I used three layers” into “three was best, here is the evidence.”
Capture lengthHow short a snippet of signal is enough? The question a real system designer asks first, and one the literature rarely answers.
Cross-channelDoes a model trained on a clean link survive a dirty one? The honest answer to “your simulation is idealised.”

That last one turned out to be the most dramatic result in the whole project.

The robustness result

Train each model on one channel, then test it on a different one. Rows are what it was trained on; columns are what it was tested on. The diagonal is the easy matched case that most papers report; the off-diagonal cells are the honest ones.

Trained onCNN → severeSVM → severe
clean channel (AWGN only)89.8 %21.8 %
realistic channel91.2 %72.0 %
severe channel (matched)97.8 %94.0 %

Read the top row. Both models were trained on a clean channel and then met a badly degraded one — which is exactly what happens when you train on a simulation and deploy on a real link.

The CNN dropped from 97.9% to 89.8%. It degraded, but it still worked.

The classical method dropped from 93.0% to 21.8%. With five formats, pure guessing scores 20%. It collapsed to almost exactly chance.

Why this happens

The 15 statistics are computed assuming a particular signal model. Change the impairments and those numbers shift, so the boundaries the classifier learned no longer point at the right regions — it fails catastrophically rather than gracefully.

The CNN learns spatial patterns in the picture. A distorted constellation still looks broadly like the right constellation, so it degrades instead of collapsing.

This is arguably the most publishable finding here, because it is a deployment claim rather than an accuracy claim: if you train on a simulation and the real link is dirtier than you assumed, the classical method fails completely while the image-based one survives.

And note the irony — this experiment is only possible because the data is simulated. Being able to dial impairments up and down turns the project’s biggest weakness into its strongest result.

How to read your own results

Four things a professor may point at and ask you to explain. Each one is simple once you know what you are looking at.

The accuracy-versus-OSNR curve

This is the main figure of the paper. One line per classifier.

  • Every curve rises to the right. More signal quality, better accuracy. If a curve went the other way, something is broken.
  • The shaded band is the spread across our three runs. It is the honesty of the figure: if two bands overlap heavily, you cannot claim one model beats the other there.
  • The dotted floor is pure guessing. With five alphabets that is 20%, not 0%. Always compare against the floor.
  • The horizontal gap between two curves at a chosen accuracy is the OSNR margin — and it is the number to quote, not the percentage difference.
Quote the margin, not the percentage

“Our CNN is 5 points more accurate” means little to an optical engineer. “Our CNN reaches 95% accuracy several dB earlier in OSNR” means something concrete: more reach, or fewer amplifiers on the route. Same result, far stronger framing.

The confusion matrix

A grid answering one question: when the true answer was X, what did the model actually say?

  • Rows are the truth. Columns are the guess. Say this out loud before explaining anything else — people mix them up constantly.
  • The diagonal is correct answers. A perfect model is dark down the diagonal and blank everywhere else.
  • Off-diagonal cells are the interesting part. They tell you which pairs get confused, not just how often.

In ours, the errors concentrate between 16-QAM and 64-QAM — the two dense square grids, which differ only in spacing. That is not a flaw; it is the confusion matrix confirming the physics you can see in Figure 1.

What “98.7 ± 0.2” means

We run every experiment three times from different random starting points. The first number is the average, the second is how much it varied.

Why bother: a single accuracy could be luck. The spread tells a reader whether a difference is trustworthy. The practical rule: if two models differ by less than their spreads, do not claim one is better. Saying that plainly about your own results is what makes a reader believe the rest of them.

The training curves

Two lines per plot: performance on data the model learned from, and on data it did not.

  • Both improving together — healthy, the model is learning a general rule.
  • Training keeps improving while validation stalls or worsens — that is overfitting, the model memorising rather than learning.

Ours shows a little of the second near the end, which is exactly why early stopping exists: it detects that moment, stops, and keeps the best earlier version. If an examiner points at that dip, the answer is not an apology — it is “that is early stopping doing its job, and here is the epoch it kept.”

10

The code, file by file

If someone says “walk me through your code”, this is the tour. Ten files, each with one job.

The shape of it

Make the data → define the models → train and measure them → run the extra studies → draw the figures → check it is all correct.

Each file does exactly one of those. That separation is deliberate: you can restyle a figure without re-running an hour of training.

The settings

config.py — every constant in the project: the random seed, the five alphabets, the noise range, the impairment levels, the training settings.

Why it exists separately: if a number appeared in two files and you changed it in only one, your results would quietly become wrong — and you might not find out until a reviewer did. One source of truth prevents that.

Making the data

data_generation.py — the simulator. Builds the five constellations, dirties the signal with the five impairments, and converts each capture into all three representations (picture, raw samples, 15 numbers).

It also runs the theory check every time, so you cannot generate data without seeing proof the physics is right.

The models

cnn_model.py — the three neural networks: the image CNN, the raw-signal CNN, and the feature MLP. Definitions only, no training.

baselines.py — the SVM and k-NN, each wrapped so that the scaling is learned from the training data only. That detail matters: computing it over all the data would leak test information into training and inflate the accuracy. It is the most common silent mistake in machine-learning papers, and reviewers check for it.

Training and measuring

train_and_evaluate.py — the main experiment. Loads the data, splits it into three parts, trains all five classifiers, measures them on held-out data, repeats for three random seeds.

Why the data is split three ways

Training set — what the model learns from.

Validation set — checked during training to decide when to stop. Because we make decisions using it, it is no longer a neutral judge.

Test set — touched exactly once, at the very end. This is the only honest number, and it is the one that goes in the paper.

The extra studies

experiments.py — the four experiments from the previous section: unseen noise levels, the architecture sweep, capture length, and cross-channel robustness.

The figures

plots.py — reads the saved results and draws everything. It does no computation at all, which is the point: you can restyle a figure for a conference template without re-running the experiments.

Checking the work

verify_project.py — independent correctness checks. It deliberately re-derives results instead of trusting the saved ones.

The most important check reloads the trained model and recomputes the headline accuracy from scratch, confirming it matches to nine decimal places. There is also a deliberately destructive test: shuffle the labels so they are meaningless, retrain, and confirm accuracy collapses to guessing. If it does not, there is a hidden leak somewhere.

This script earned its keep

It initially failed two of its own noise checks. Investigating showed the check was wrong, not the data — it was measuring a tiny noise value by subtracting two nearly identical large numbers, which amplifies rounding error enormously. Measuring the noise directly instead fixed it, and the tolerance could then be made stricter.

Worth remembering as a principle: when a test fails, find out which side is broken before assuming.

Running it all

run_remaining.py — chains every remaining stage into one unattended run. If one stage crashes the rest still run, finished stages are skipped on a restart, and everything is logged.

paper/make_macros.py — writes every number from the results straight into the manuscript. No number is typed into the paper by hand. Re-run an experiment, re-run this, recompile — the text can never contradict your own tables. That is where papers get their embarrassing errors.

The order to run them

#CommandTime
1python data_generation.py --n-per-class 600~5 min
2python train_and_evaluate.py --seeds 42 43 44~60 min
3python experiments.py all~2 hrs
4python plots.py~1 min
5python verify_project.py~2 min

Or simply python run_remaining.py, which does steps 2 to 5 by itself.

If asked “did you write this yourself?”

Answer honestly. What matters is whether you can explain why each choice was made — why three convolution layers, why the data is split three ways, why the scaling is fitted on training data only. This guide exists so that you can. Understanding your project is the real requirement; pretending is not.

11

Answering the examiner

The questions most likely to come up, with answers you can say out loud. Click any one to open it.

What is OSNR, in one sentence?

The ratio of optical signal power to noise power, in decibels, measured in a standard 0.1 nm reference bandwidth. High OSNR means a clean signal.

Why does accuracy fall at low OSNR?

Because the information genuinely is not there any more. Noise smears each point into a cloud, and once neighbouring clouds overlap, the structure that separates one alphabet from another is physically erased.

No classifier can recover information the channel destroyed. That is a fundamental limit, not a shortcoming of our model — and Figure 1 shows it directly.

What is the actual contribution of your paper?

Existing work shows that neural networks on constellation images beat classical methods on statistical features. But that comparison changes two variables at once — the method becomes deep and the input becomes an image.

We separate them by adding a neural network trained on the same 15 features. Three very different algorithms on identical features perform almost identically, while changing the representation produces a large gain. So the representation is the dominant design variable, not model capacity. That is a more transferable conclusion than “neural networks are better”, because it tells other researchers where to spend their effort.

Isn’t it unfair to give the CNN more information?

That is the finding, not a flaw in the experiment. Both models see the same physical signal; they differ only in how it is represented. Feeding hand-crafted features to a classical classifier is exactly how this was done before deep learning, so it is the correct thing to compare against.

The claim is not “CNNs are better algorithms” — it is “learning from the constellation image beats summarising it with hand-picked statistics.” Same signals, same training set, same test set.

Why did the raw-signal model do worst? It has the most information.

Information is not the same as accessible structure. The symbols are independent, so their order in time carries almost nothing about the alphabet — but a one-dimensional convolution is built specifically to exploit order.

The network has to estimate a distribution from a sequence. The two-dimensional histogram already is a density estimate, so the binning step performs work the network would otherwise have to learn.

Why is your data simulated? Is that not a weakness?

It is a limitation and we state it plainly — but simulation is the correct choice here, not a compromise. Our paper is an accuracy-versus-OSNR curve across 21 exact noise levels for five alphabets. Only simulation gives a controlled sweep like that with guaranteed correct labels. Real measurements drift, and the equipment costs crores.

It is also standard practice in this field. And critically, I validated the simulator against exact theory to within 0.004 before training anything — so I can prove my data is physically correct, which someone using a downloaded dataset usually cannot.

The genuine limitation is realism, and the cross-channel robustness experiment measures exactly that.

Which alphabets get confused, and why?

The dense square grids — 16-QAM and 64-QAM. QPSK has only four widely spaced points and stays readable deep into the noise. 8-QAM (star) and 32-QAM (cross) have distinctive non-square shapes, which helps them. 16-QAM and 64-QAM differ only in grid spacing, so once noise blurs the grid they look alike.

Why normalise every constellation to the same power?

Otherwise the alphabets would differ in brightness and any classifier could identify them by measuring power alone. That would be meaningless, because a real receiver applies automatic gain control and always delivers a fixed power. Normalising forces the model to learn shape, which is the actual physics.

Is OSNR the same as SNR?

No, and the difference is often glossed over. OSNR is optical, measured in a fixed 0.1 nm (12.5 GHz) reference bandwidth across both polarisations. SNR is electrical, after the receiver filters to its own symbol rate and picks one polarisation.

For our 32 GBaud single-polarisation system, SNR in dB equals OSNR in dB minus 1.07 dB. Reporting against OSNR without that conversion overstates your electrical SNR by about a decibel.

Why only three convolution layers? Why not a big network like ResNet?

Capacity should match the problem. Our images are synthetic, greyscale and uncluttered, and the pattern to recognise is a simple repeating grid. ResNet’s twenty-five million settings exist for photographs of the real world.

Also, after three pooling stages a single element of the feature map already covers roughly a thirty-by-thirty region of the input — the scale of the pattern itself. And we did not just assert this: the architecture sweep tests it, and a fourth layer gains nothing.

What is overfitting and how did you prevent it?

Overfitting is when a model memorises its training examples instead of learning the general rule — training accuracy keeps rising while validation accuracy stalls or falls.

Four defences: dropout (randomly ignoring 30% of features during training so the model cannot lean on any one), weight decay, early stopping, and a large varied dataset. The training-curve figure is the evidence that they worked.

Why report a range rather than a single accuracy?

Because a single number could be luck. We repeat every experiment with three different random starting points and report the mean and spread. That lets a reader judge whether a difference between two models is real. If two models’ ranges overlap heavily, you cannot claim one is better — and saying so honestly is what makes the rest believable.

Could this work on real experimental data?

The pipeline transfers, but the model would need retraining or fine-tuning on real captures — and our robustness experiment shows exactly why: a model trained on a clean channel loses accuracy on a dirtier one. Real signals also carry effects we did not simulate, such as fibre nonlinearity and polarisation multiplexing. That is the honest next step.

Glossary

Modulation formatThe “alphabet” of light patterns used to carry data. QPSK, 16-QAM and so on.
MFIModulation Format Identification — working out which alphabet is arriving, from the signal alone.
OPMOptical Performance Monitoring — judging a link’s health from the received signal. MFI is one part of it.
Constellation diagramThe picture you get by plotting every received symbol by its brightness and phase.
SymbolOne transmitted pattern; one dot on the constellation diagram.
I and QThe two independent components of the light wave, plotted on the two axes. In-phase and Quadrature.
OSNROptical Signal-to-Noise Ratio, in decibels. High means clean.
ASE noiseThe random light that optical amplifiers add along with the boost. The dominant noise on long links.
CumulantA statistical quantity built so that Gaussian noise cancels out. The classical fingerprint of a constellation.
CNNConvolutional Neural Network — the standard model for recognising patterns in images.
MLPMultilayer Perceptron — a plain neural network. Ours is the critical control experiment.
SVMSupport Vector Machine — a classical classifier that finds the widest boundary between classes.
k-NNk-Nearest Neighbours — classify by finding the most similar past examples and taking a vote.
OverfittingWhen a model memorises training examples instead of learning the general rule.
EpochOne complete pass through the training data.
BaudSymbols per second. Our system runs at 32 GBaud — 32 billion symbols each second.
